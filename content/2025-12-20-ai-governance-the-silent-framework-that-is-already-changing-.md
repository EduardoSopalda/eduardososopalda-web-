A few weeks ago, a colleague from the U.S. asked me half curious, half horrified, like someone watching a slow-motion compliance documentary, how on earth it was possible to work with AI in Europe “under such restrictive rules”. She genuinely looked concerned. If she’d had pearls, she would have clutched them. For a moment I could almost hear the echo of legal terms bouncing off invisible walls.

I smiled and asked her something back, because it felt fair: how do you do it? I mean, how do you build trust with customers using a technology this powerful… without guardrails? We both laughed. A polite laugh. The kind of laugh where both people understand that the joke is funny, but the situation behind it isn’t. It's funny because it's true

For years, AI lived in a world almost entirely defined by speed. Everything revolved around time-to-market, endless experimentation, prototypes rushed into existence with the promise of instant transformation. If it shimmered even a bit, it was immediately “the future”. Companies didn’t just adopt AI; they sprinted toward it like it was a golden ticket. The important thing was to be fast, not necessarily right.And yet, almost quietly, a shift started happening. Not because innovation slowed down innovation, if anything, is as frenetic as ever but because reality began to show something uncomfortable: AI without governance doesn’t scale. It doesn’t build trust. And very often, it doesn’t survive long enough to matter.

Meanwhile, this change isn’t coming from philosophical debate rooms. It’s happening in real life. Countries like India and China already regulate algorithmic systems. Several U.S. states are drawing lines around transparency, fairness and responsible AI use. And Europe… well, Europe did what Europe tends to do: it designed a whole structure around it. The EU AI Act entered into force in 2024 and is rolling out between 2025 and 2027, not as a distant ideal, but as a framework that says something very simple and very firm: trust, safety and accountability are not accessories. They are now part of the architecture.

And yes, consequences are real. We’re not talking about symbolic warnings. We’re talking about potential fines reaching up to 7% of global annual turnover. Governance is no longer a legal footnote buried somewhere after section 23. It has moved to the front row. Suddenly, governance is strategy.There is another reality that rarely makes flashy headlines but should: most AI never reaches production. Depending on the source, you’ll hear numbers hovering between 80% and 87% of initiatives that either stall, crumble, or simply fail to deliver what they promised. Not because the teams weren’t brilliant. Not because the technology wasn’t impressive enough. The real reasons tend to be far less glamorous: lack of control, missing traceability, fragile data quality, compliance risks sitting quietly in the shadows, and projects that somehow lost alignment with the business somewhere along the way.

Inside organizations, something is changing. What once was a race to launch first is starting to look more like a race to build responsibly. Companies are beginning to value robustness over spectacle, predictability over clever improvisation, sustainability over quick applause. It’s a shift from “look what we built” to “look what we can maintain without fear”. Less magic. More engineering. Still fascinating, just a bit more grown-up.

That’s where AI sandboxes appear, not as bureaucratic punishment chambers, but as incredibly practical solutions. They are controlled environments where you can experiment, test, validate and breathe before pushing a model into the real world. Think of them as the responsible version of curiosity: you still explore, still innovate, but without playing roulette with trust, safety or reputation.

So maybe the big question isn’t whether regulation will slow innovation. Maybe the real question is: will governance finally help more AI projects actually cross the bridge into reality and stay there?

And if there’s interest, maybe in a next post we can talk about how to design that governance in a way that feels lightweight, practical, and aligned with the spirit of the EU AI Act… without killing creativity in the process. Because the goal has never been to slow down AI. It’s to make sure it’s worth accelerating.
